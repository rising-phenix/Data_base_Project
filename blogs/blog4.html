Why Most Designers Are Still Getting Typography Wrong in 2026

Like Blog | Designing

Walk through any portfolio site today and you will notice the same problem repeating itself across hundreds of otherwise beautiful designs. The layouts are clean. The color palettes are considered. But the typography? Still an afterthought. Still broken in the same familiar ways. And in 2025, that is no longer excusable.

The Hierarchy Problem Nobody Talks About

Most designers understand typographic hierarchy in theory big headline, medium subheading, small body copy. But real hierarchy is not just about size. It is about weight contrast, spacing, color temperature, and the visual tension between elements. When all of those are working together, a user knows exactly where to look without thinking about it. When they are not, the page feels flat and the user hesitates.

The fix is rarely adding more styles. It is usually removing them. Limit yourself to two typefaces maximum. Pick one for display, one for body. Everything else weight, size, spacing does the heavy lifting.

Line Length Is Quietly Ruining Your Readability

The optimal line length for comfortable reading sits between 50 and 75 characters per line. That is not an opinion, it is backed by decades of readability research. Yet scroll through almost any modern website built on a full width grid and you will find body text stretching 120 characters across a widescreen monitor. The reader's eye loses its place. Fatigue sets in. People stop reading.

The solution is embarrassingly simple: constrain your text container. A max width of around 680 pixels on body copy is a rule that never fails. Your content deserves to actually be read, not just scrolled past.

The Return of Editorial Design Thinking

Something interesting is happening in the design industry right now. After years of everything converging on the same clean, minimal, card-based layouts, the best studios and agencies are pulling inspiration from print specifically from magazine and editorial design of the 1960s through 1990s.

Bold type at unexpected angles. Text wrapping around irregular shapes. Columns that break their own grid. Oversized pull quotes that bleed into margins. These are not retro gimmicks. They are solutions to a real problem: digital design became so predictable that users stopped noticing it. Breaking the grid only works when you fully understand the grid first.

Whitespace Is Not Empty Space

Junior designers fill space. Senior designers protect it. Whitespace is one of the most misunderstood tools in design because it feels like doing nothing, when in reality it is doing everything. It creates breathing room, directs attention, communicates premium quality, and separates a polished interface from a cluttered one.

The current trend of cramming more sections, more icons, more feature callouts onto a single page is a client-driven problem that designers need to push back on. Three things communicated clearly will always outperform ten things communicated poorly. That is a conversation worth having in every project brief.

Color Consistency Is a System, Not a Vibe

Picking a nice palette is the easy part. The hard part is using it consistently across every state, every component, every screen at every size. The designers doing this well right now are not just choosing colors — they are building color systems with clearly defined roles. Primary actions. Destructive states. Neutral surfaces. Semantic feedback colors. Each one with a purpose, documented and reusable.

Without a system, every new screen becomes a decision. With one, it becomes a choice you already made. The best design work is invisible because every decision was deliberate.

What Separates Good Designers Right Now

The design industry in 2025 is more competitive than it has ever been. Portfolios are polished. Tools are accessible. But the designers standing out are not the ones with the most trendy mockups they are the ones who can articulate why every decision was made. The thinking behind the kerning. The reasoning behind the color choice. The logic behind the layout.

Design has always been problem solving dressed up as aesthetics. The fundamentals — hierarchy, spacing, contrast, consistency have not changed and they never will. Master those and every trend becomes a tool rather than a trap.
